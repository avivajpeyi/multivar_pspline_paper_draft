\begin{abstract}
We present a Bayesian multivariate P-spline method for estimating the frequency-dependent cross-spectral density matrix of stationary vector time series.
The spectral matrix is parameterized via a frequency-varying Cholesky factor with log-diagonal terms to enforce Hermitian positive definiteness, while smoothness is induced through B-spline bases with difference-penalty priors. 
Inference proceeds under the Whittle likelihood using both stochastic gradient variational Bayes and MCMC for calibration. 
The approach scales linearly with the number of frequencies and basis functions. 
On synthetic VAR(2) benchmarks with known ground truth, the method accurately recovers PSD- and cross-spectral structure, yields well-calibrated credible intervals, and achieves lower relative integrated absolute error (RIAE) as sample size grows, with performance largely robust over a broad range of spline basis sizes. 
We illustrate relevance to LISA by estimating the multichannel noise spectral matrix for time-delay interferometry combinations.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{gravitational waves, PSD estimation, CSD estimation, P-splines, LISA}

\section{\label{sec:intro}Introduction}

LISA will provide three correlated data streams from time-delay interferometry combinations. 
Accurate characterization of the noise spectral density matrix $\mathbf{S}(f)$ is essential for optimal signal detection and parameter estimation in this multivariate setting.

LISA data exhibits several characteristics that challenge conventional spectral estimation methods: frequency-dependent cross-correlations between channels, non-stationary noise properties due to orbital motion, and overlapping astrophysical signals across the millihertz band. 
Our approach addresses some of these challenges by modeling the full spectral density matrix with smooth, frequency-varying P-splines under a Cholesky parameterization that guarantees Hermitian positive definiteness at every frequency. 
A hierarchical shrinkage prior provides automatic regularization.
Inference uses the Whittle likelihood with No-U-Turn sampling (NUTS) for MCMC.

The estimated spectral density matrices serve multiple roles in LISA data analysis. 
They provide noise weighting for matched filtering operations and support background characterization for targeted source analyses. 
The Bayesian framework naturally propagates spectral uncertainties to downstream global-fit parameter estimation procedures.
Integration with LISA's global analysis pipeline requires spectral estimates across multiple time scales, from hourly monitoring to months-long campaigns. 
The computational footprint for this method is predictable: for a fixed number of channels, the dominant operations scale approximately as $O(nK)$ with the length of the timeseries $n$ and spline basis size $K$, enabling routine re-estimation as data accumulates.

Implementation of this methodology is expected to improve LISA's detection sensitivity through more accurate noise characterization and reduce systematic errors in parameter estimation by properly accounting for inter-channel correlations. 
These improvements directly impact LISA's primary science objectives, including the detection of massive black hole mergers, galactic binary systems, and potential primordial gravitational wave backgrounds.


\section{Multivariate P-splines}


\subsection{Likelihood}
Assume we are given a time series of gravitational wave observations of length $n$ from $p$ channels. To set notation,
let $\Z=(\Z_1,\ldots,\Z_n)^\intercal\in  \mathbb{R}^{n\times p}$ be a $p$-dimensional stationary, mean-zero time series, sampled at time intervals $\Delta_t=1/(2f_{Ny})$, i.e., $\Z_\nu=\Z(\nu\Delta_t)\in \mathbb{R}^p$ for $l=1,\ldots,n$, where $f_{Ny}$ is the Nyquist frequency, for a total observation time of $T$ with a total of $n=T/\Delta_t$ sampled values for each of the $p$ dimensions. The frequency resolution, \(\Delta_f\), is given by
\begin{align}
\Delta_f = \frac{1}{n \Delta_t} = \frac{1}{T}\, .
\end{align}
Let $\d_k=(d_1(f_k), d_2(f_k), \ldots, d_p(f_k))^\intercal$ be the Discrete Fourier transform {(DFT)} of $\Z$ given by
\begin{align}
\d(f_k) = \Delta_t\sum_{t=1}^{n} \Z_t\exp \left(-2\pi i \frac{k}{n} t \right)\, ,
\end{align}
where $f_k= k \Delta_f= k/({n\Delta_t})=k /{T}$ for $k=1,\ldots, N$, and $N = n/2$ if $n$ is even, $N = (n-1)/2$ if $n$ is odd. 

For stationary time series with absolutely summable autocovariances, i.e., $\sum_{h=1}^\infty\gamma_{lm}(h)<\infty$, the discrete Fourier coefficients $\d(f_k)$ %(multiplied by $1 / \sqrt{n}$)
are asymptotically independent and have a complex Gaussian distribution with mean zero and  covariance matrix $T\, \S(f_k)$ with  the continuous two-sided spectral density matrix 
\[\S(f)=\frac{1}{2f_{Ny}}
\sum_{\nu=-\infty}^{\infty} \Gamma(\nu \Delta_t) \exp \left(-2\pi i f \nu\Delta_t \right),\] %
the Fourier transform of the time-invariant autocovariance function $\Gamma(h)=\left( \gamma_{lm}(h)\right)=\mathbb{E}(\Z_{t}\Z_{t+h}^\intercal)$ that in turn defines the Toeplitz covariance matrix of $\Z$. This asymptotic Gaussian distribution is the basis of the multivariate Whittle likelihood approximation in the frequency domain, expressed as ...


The multivariate Whittle likelihood is given by
\begin{align}\label{eq:Whittle likelihood}
 \mathcal{L}(\d|\S) &\propto  \prod_{k=1}^{N} \det(\S(f_k))^{-1} \times \nonumber \\
 & \exp\left(-\frac{1}{T}\d(f_k)^* \S(f_k)^{-1} \d(f_k)\right),
\end{align}
where $\d(f_k)^*$ is the conjugate transpose of the $\d(f_k)$ and $\S(f_k)$ is a $p \times p$ Hermitian positive semidefinite spectral density matrix at each $f_k$. 

 We divide the time series into $N_{b}$ equal-sized blocks $\Z=(\Z^{(1)},\ldots,\Z^{(N_{b})})^\intercal $ with each block a $p$-dimensional time series each of length $n/N_{b}$. The discrete Fourier transform of each block is denoted by 
$\d^{(i)}$, $i=1,\ldots,N_{b}$. 
Assuming stationarity of the time series and independence among different blocks, the overall blocked likelihood is the product of the individual Whittle likelihoods of each block,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:block_lnl}
    \mathcal{L}_b(\d|\S) = \prod^{N_b}_{i=1} \mathcal{L}(\d^{(i)}|\S) \ .
\end{equation}
Now, the blocked Whittle likelihood can be simplified by using the  trace rotation rule to\\
%for the product of  square matrices (where the trace of a matrix is the sum of its diagonal elements, i.e., $\textrm{tr}(A)=\sum_{i=1}^n a_{ii}$), namely 
%$\tr(A B C)=\tr(BC A)=\tr(C A B)$. 
$\mathcal{L}(\d^{(i)}|\S)$
\begin{eqnarray*}
&\propto & \prod_{k=1}^{N_l}   \left|\S(f_k)\right|^{-N_b}  
  \exp\left(- \sum_{i=1}^{N_b}   \tr\left[\d^{(i)}(f_k)^* \S(f_k)^{-1} \d^{(i)}(f_k)\right]\right)\\
  &\propto &  \prod_{k=1}^{N_l}    \left|\S(f_k)\right|^{-N_b}   
  \exp\left(-   \tr\left[ \S(f_k)^{-1} \sum_{i=1}^{N_b} \underbrace{\d^{(i)}(f_k)\d^{(i)}(f_k)^*}_{\I^{(i)}(f_k)} \right]\right)\\ 
   &\propto & \prod_{k=1}^{N_l}     \left|\S(f_k)\right|^{-N_b}   
  \exp\left(-   \tr\left[ \S(f_k)^{-1} N_b\bar{\I}(f_k)\right]\right)\\
   &\propto &  \prod_{k=1}^{N_l}    \left|\S(f_k)\right|^{-N_b}   
  \exp\left(-   \tr\left[ \S(f_k)^{-1} \Y(f_k)\right]\right)
\end{eqnarray*}
where $\displaystyle \bar{\I}(f)=\frac{1}{N_b} \sum_{i=1}^{N_b} \I^{(i)}(f)$ is the block-averaged periodogram (or Welch estimate of the spectral density with non-overlapping blocks) of length $N_l=\frac{n}{2N_b}$ ,  $\I^{(i)}(f)=\d^{(i)}(f)\d^{(i)}(f)^*$ the periodogram matrix of the $i$th block at frequency $f$, and 
$\Y(f)=N_b \bar{\I}(f)$.
Therefore, $\Y(f)$ has a $d$-dimensional complex Wishart distribution\footnote{ We use the notation $\A \sim {\cal CW}_d(\M,m)$  if  the $d\times d$ Hermitian positive definite random matrix $\A$ has a complex Wishart distribution with mean $m\M$ and $m$ degrees of freedom. Its density is proportional to $\det(\M)^{-m}\exp(-\tr[\M^{-1}\A])$.} with $N_b$ degrees of freedom.
This is consistent with the independent $d$-dimensional complex Wishart distribution with one degree of freedom for each individual periodogram, i.e.,
\[\I^{(i)}(f_k)\sim {\cal CW}_d(\S(f_k),1). \]
Using the eigendecomposition\\ $\Y(f_k)=\sum_{\nu=1}^p \lambda^{(k)}_\nu \v^{(k)}_\nu\v^{(k)*}_\nu=\sum_{\nu=1}^p  \u^{(k)}_\nu\u^{(k)*}_\nu$ with $\u^{(k)}_\nu=\sqrt{\lambda^{(k)}_l}\v^{(k)}_\nu$, 
the likelihood function can be expressed as
\begin{eqnarray}\label{eq:log-like}
  &\propto &   \prod_{k=1}^{N_l} \left|\S(f_k)\right|^{-N_b} \exp\left(- \tr\left[ \S(f_k)^{-1} \Y(f_k)\right]\right)\\
  &\propto &   \prod_{k=1}^{N_l} \left|\S(f_k)\right|^{-N_b} \exp\left(- \sum_{\nu=1}^p\u_\nu^{(k)*}\S(f_k)^{-1}\u_\nu^{(k)}\right)
 % &\propto &  \prod_{k=1}^{N_l} \left|\S(f_k)\right|^{-N_b} \prod_{\nu=1}^p %\exp\left(-\u_\nu^{(k)*}\S(f_k)^{-1}\u_\nu^{(k)}\right)
\end{eqnarray}


\subsection{Parameterization using the Cholesky Decomposition}
Following the approach of \citet{RosenOri2007Aeom,Hu2023}, we parameterize the inverse spectral density matrix using its Cholesky decomposition. Specifically, we write $\bold{S}(f_k)^{-1} = \bold{T}_k^* \bold{D}_k^{-1} \bold{T}_k$, where $\bold{D}_k$ is a diagonal matrix with positive diagonal elements $\delta_{1k}^2, \delta_{2k}^2, \ldots, \delta_{pk}^2$ and
\begin{align}
\bold{T}_k = \begin{pmatrix}
1 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\
-\theta_{21}^{(k)} & 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\
-\theta_{31}^{(k)} & -\theta_{32}^{(k)} & 1 & \ddots & \vdots \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \ddots & 0 \\
-\theta_{p1}^{(k)} & -\theta_{p2}^{(k)}& \cdots & -\theta_{p,p-1}^{(k)} & 1
\end{pmatrix}
\end{align}
is a $p \times p$ complex unit lower triangular matrix with $\theta_{il}^{(k)}$ representing the complex-valued off-diagonal elements for $i > l$.


This parameterization allows us to rewrite the likelihood as the product of $p$ likelihoods $\mathcal{L}_j$ parameterised by $\btheta_j,\bdelta_j$, for $j=1,\ldots,p$,
\begin{align}
 \mathcal{L}(\d|\S)
 \propto \prod_{j=1}^{p} \mathcal{L}_j(\u_j,\u_{<j}|{\btheta_j,\bdelta_j}),
 \label{eq:likelihood}
\end{align}
where
\begin{align}
\mathcal{L}_j(&\u_j,\u_{<j}|\btheta_j,\bdelta_j) \propto \nonumber \\
&  \prod_{k=1}^{N} \delta_{jk}^{-2N
_b} \exp \left(\frac{-\sum_{\nu=1}^p\left|u_{j\nu}^{(k)}-\sum_{l=1}^{j-1}\theta_{jl}^{(k)}u_{l\nu}^{(k)} \right|^2}{T\delta_{jk}^2} \right)
\label{eq:likelihoodj}
\end{align}%
and $\u_{<j}^{(k)}$ denotes the components of the vectors $\u_\nu^{(k)}$, $\nu=1,\ldots,p$, preceding the $j$th component, the parameters $\btheta_j,\bdelta_j$ represent the set of $\theta_k$ and $\delta_k$ for the $j$th component of the multivariate time series, $\theta_{jl}^{(k)}$ represents the corresponding element in the matrix $T_k$ for any $j>l$, and $\theta_{jl}^{(k)} = 1$ when $j=l$. %$d_j(f_k)$ denotes the Fourier coefficient of the $j$th component, and $\d_j=(d_j(f_1),\ldots,d_j(f_N))^\intercal$.
Explicitly, if we have 3 channels as in the LISA case, we have 3 independent likelihoods ${\mathcal L}_j$ that depend on subvectors $\bdelta_1$, $(\bdelta_2,\btheta_{21})$, and $(\bdelta_3,\btheta_{31},\btheta_{32})$ of the total parameter vector:
\begin{align}
\mathcal{L}_1(&\u_1|\bdelta_1) \propto \nonumber \\
&  \prod_{k=1}^{N} \delta_{1k}^{-2N_b} \exp \left(\frac{-\sum_{\nu=1}^p\left|u_{1\nu}^{(k)}\right|^2}{T\delta_{1k}^2} \right)
\label{eq:likelihood1}
\end{align}%
\begin{align}
\mathcal{L}_2(&\u_1,\u_2|\btheta_{21},\bdelta_2) \propto \nonumber \\
&  \prod_{k=1}^{N} \delta_{2k}^{-2N_b} \exp \left(\frac{-\sum_{\nu=1}^p\left|u_{2\nu}^{(k)}-\theta_{21}^{(k)}u_{1\nu}^{(k)} \right|^2}{T\delta_{2k}^2} \right)
\label{eq:likelihood2}
\end{align}%
\begin{align}
\mathcal{L}_3(&\u_1,\u_2,\u_3|\btheta_{31},\btheta_{32},\bdelta_3) \propto \nonumber \\
&  \prod_{k=1}^{N} \delta_{3k}^{-2N_b} \exp \left(\frac{-\sum_{\nu=1}^p\left|u_{3\nu}^{(k)}-\theta_{31}^{(k)}u_{1\nu}^{(k)} -\theta_{32}^{(k)}u_{2\nu}^{(k)}\right|^2}{T\delta_{3k}^2} \right)
\label{eq:likelihood3}
\end{align}%
This means, we can run an MCMC chain for each posterior of $\bdelta_1$, $(\bdelta_2,\btheta_{21})$, and $(\bdelta_3,\btheta_{31},\btheta_{32})$ separately and in parallel which should reduce the computation time.

\subsection{(Renate's version)Coarse--graining the frequency grid}
\label{subsec:coarse}
Starting from equation (5), one could further coarse-grain' the frequencies by dividing the set of Fourier frequencies into $N_c$ disjoint subsets $J_h$, i.e., $\{f_1,\ldots,f_{N_l}\}=\cup_{h=1}^{N_c} J_h$, where each $J_h$ contains $N_h+1$ frequencies and $\bar{f}_h$ denotes the midpoint Fourier frequency of interval $J_h$.
Instead of each individual $\Y(f_k), k=1,\ldots,N_l$, we use $\bar{\Y}_h=\sum_{f\in J_h} \Y(f)$, for $h=1,\ldots, N_c$. As the $\Y(f)$ are asymptotically independent and complex Wishart distributed, i.e., $\Y(f)\sim {\cal CW}(\S(f), N_b)$, the distribution of $\Y_h$ can be approximated by ${\cal CW}(\S(\bar{f}_h), N_b(N_h+1))$, but only under the assumption that the spectral density matrices $\S(f)$ for $f\in J_h$ are very close. (Note that the sum of independent complex Wishart distributions is complex Wishart but only if the scale matrices are identical. If they differ, the distribution can only  be characterised via its Laplace transform but does not have a closed-form density. [This is a mistake in Quentin's JCAP paper])
So it is important to choose the length of the frequency intervals such that they don't contain peaks or abrupt changes of the spectrum.
Then the likelihood in equation (\ref{eq:log-like}) can be approximated by
\begin{eqnarray}\label{eq:log-like}
  &\propto &   \prod_{h=1}^{N_c} \left|\S(\bar{f}_h)\right|^{-N_b(N_h+1)} \exp\left(- \tr\left[ \S(\bar{f}_h)^{-1} \bar{\Y}_h\right]\right)\\
 % &\propto &   \prod_{k=1}^{N_l} \left|\S(f_k)\right|^{-N_b} \exp\left(- %\sum_{\nu=1}^p\u_\nu^{(k)*}\S(f_k)^{-1}\u_\nu^{(k)}\right)
  &\propto &  \prod_{h=1}^{N_c} \left|\S(\bar{f}_h)\right|^{-N_b(N_h+1)}  \exp\left(\sum_{\nu=1}^p-\u_\nu^{(h)*}\S(\bar{f}_h)^{-1}\u_\nu^{(h)}\right)
\end{eqnarray}
where we use the eigendecomposition of $\bar{\Y}_h=\sum_{\nu=1}^p \lambda^{(h)}_\nu \v^{(h)}_\nu\v^{(h)*}_\nu=\sum_{\nu=1}^p  \u^{(h)}_\nu\u^{(h)*}_\nu$ with $\u^{(h)}_\nu=\sqrt{\lambda^{(h)}_l}\v^{(h)}_\nu$. 


    

\subsection{Coarse--graining the frequency grid}
\label{subsec:coarse}

For long data segments or high sampling rates, the Fourier grid $\{f_k\}_{k=1}^N$ can be extremely fine, making a full Whittle likelihood evaluation unnecessarily expensive in spectral regions where $\mathbf{S}(f)$ varies slowly. 
We therefore adopt a \emph{coarse--graining} strategy that preserves the Whittle likelihood structure while reducing the number of frequency evaluations.

Following \citet{Caprini_2019} (\textsc{SGWBINNER}), we aggregate neighbouring Fourier ordinates into logarithmically spaced bins at high frequencies and evaluate the model only at one representative frequency per bin. 
Each bin’s contribution to the likelihood is weighted by its number of member frequencies. 
The low--frequency region is left unbinned to retain resolution where the spectrum typically varies most rapidly.

\paragraph*{Univariate case.}

Let $\{f_k\}_{k=1}^N$ denote the original Fourier grid and $I(f_k)$ the (one--sided) periodogram. 
Below a transition frequency $f_\mathrm{tr}$ we retain every frequency (full resolution). 
Above $f_\mathrm{tr}$ we partition the grid into logarithmically spaced bins $b=1,\ldots,B$, each with representative frequency $\bar f_b$ and cardinality $K_b = \#\{k \in b\}$.

For a smooth spectral density $S(f)$, the sum of Whittle contributions within bin $b$,
\begin{equation}
\sum_{k\in b}\!\left\{\log S(f_k) + \frac{I(f_k)}{S(f_k)}\right\},
\end{equation}
is well approximated by
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:coarse_univar}
K_b \log S(\bar f_b)
\;+\;
K_b \frac{\bar I_b}{S(\bar f_b)}\,,
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
\bar I_b
\equiv
\frac{1}{K_b}\sum_{k\in b} I(f_k)\,.
\end{equation}

This defines a coarse grid $\{\bar f_b\}$ with weights $K_b$ and bin--mean periodogram values $\bar I_b$. 
Replacing the fine--grid sum by the coarsened version gives the approximate Whittle likelihood
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:coarse_univar_ll}
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{d}|\mathbf{S})
\propto
\prod_{b=1}^{B}
\!
\left[\det\mathbf{S}(\bar f_b)\right]^{-K_b}
\exp\!\left(
-\frac{K_b}{T}\,
\mathbf{d}(\bar f_b)^{*}
\mathbf{S}(\bar f_b)^{-1}
\mathbf{d}(\bar f_b)
\right),
\end{equation}
which is exact if $S(f)$ is constant within each bin, and remains accurate when $S(f)$ varies slowly across logarithmically spaced bins.

\paragraph*{Multivariate (Cholesky) case.}

\avi{This is majorly a work in progress}
As in Section~\ref{sec:likelihood}, we denote by 
\(\mathbf{d}(f_k) = (d_1(f_k), \ldots, d_p(f_k))^\top\)
the complex DFT vector of the $p$-channel time series. 
For the multivariate Whittle likelihood written in its Cholesky form, averaging the complex DFT vectors within a bin is not sufficient, since the quadratic term depends on second--order products $\mathbf{d}(f_k)\mathbf{d}(f_k)^{*}$. 
To retain exactness while evaluating the model at a single frequency per bin, we instead aggregate bin  statistics that capture all second--order cross--products relevant to the likelihood.

For channel (row) $j$ and bin $b$ with members $k\in b$, define
\begin{align}
c_j(b) &= \sum_{k\in b} |d_j(f_k)|^2, \\
v_{j\nu}(b) &= \sum_{k\in b} d_j(f_k)\, d_\nu(f_k)^{*}, \qquad (\nu < j), \\
G_{j\nu m}(b) &= \sum_{k\in b} d_\nu(f_k)\, d_m(f_k)^{*}, \qquad (\nu,m < j), \\
K_b &= \#\{k \in b\}.
\label{eq:coarse_stats}
\end{align}

Here $c_j(b)$ is a scalar, $v_j(b)\in\mathbb{C}^{j-1}$, and $G_j(b)\in\mathbb{C}^{(j-1)\times(j-1)}$ is Hermitian. 
These quantities are the frequency--summed cross--products appearing in the quadratic form of the Whittle likelihood and can be accumulated in a single pass over the DFTs.

Let $\boldsymbol{\theta}_j(\bar f_b)\in\mathbb{C}^{j-1}$ denote the vector of off--diagonal Cholesky coefficients for row $j$ evaluated at $\bar f_b$, and let $\delta_j(\bar f_b)^2$ be the corresponding diagonal variance. 
The exact bin contribution to the row--$j$ log--Whittle likelihood is then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:coarse_block}
K_b \log \delta_j(\bar f_b)^2
\;+\;
\delta_j(\bar f_b)^{-2}
\Bigl(
\boldsymbol{\theta}_j^{*} G_j(b)\, \boldsymbol{\theta}_j
- 2\,\Re\{\boldsymbol{\theta}_j^{*} v_j(b)\}
+ c_j(b)
\Bigr).
\end{equation}

Summing \eqref{eq:coarse_block} over bins and rows recovers the full Whittle objective in terms of per--bin sufficient statistics and a coarse evaluation grid $\{\bar f_b\}$. 
In other words, we trade $O(N)$ pointwise evaluations for $O(B)$ bin evaluations ($B \ll N$) \emph{without discarding within--bin spectral information}. 
The only approximation arises from replacing 
\begin{equation}
\sum_{k\in b}\log\delta_j(f_k)^2
\quad\text{by}\quad
K_b \log\delta_j(\bar f_b)^2,
\end{equation}
which is negligible when $\delta_j(f)$ varies smoothly within each bin.

\paragraph*{Implementation details.}

We (i) retain all frequencies below a user--chosen transition $f_\mathrm{tr}$, 
(ii) partition $[f_\mathrm{tr}, f_\mathrm{max}]$ into logarithmically spaced bins, 
(iii) precompute $K_b$ and the sufficient statistics in \eqref{eq:coarse_stats} for each bin, and 
(iv) evaluate the spline fields $\log\delta_j^2(f)$ and $\theta_{j\nu}(f)$ only at $\bar f_b$. 
The coarse likelihood is then obtained by replacing the per--frequency sum with a per--bin sum, weighting the log--determinant terms by $K_b$ and using \eqref{eq:coarse_univar} or \eqref{eq:coarse_block} for the quadratic contributions. 
Low--frequency points use $K_b=1$ and are unchanged.

\paragraph*{Computational complexity.}

Let $N$ be the number of fine frequencies and $B$ the number of coarse elements ($B = N_\mathrm{low} + N_\mathrm{bins}$). 
Precomputing the bin statistics requires $O(Np^2)$ operations (a single pass over the DFTs). 
Each likelihood evaluation thereafter scales as the unbinned case but with $N$ replaced by $B$, i.e.
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{O}(Bp^2),
\end{equation}
for the quadratic forms, plus the cost of evaluating the spline fields, approximately $\mathcal{O}(BK)$ per parameter family (where $K$ is the number of basis functions). 
For typical settings $B \ll N$, runtime is reduced by roughly $N/B$ while preserving the same likelihood structure.



\subsection{P-spline Modeling}
We model the frequency-dependent parameters using penalized B-splines. Specifically, we represent:

\begin{align}
\log \delta_{kj}^2 &= \sum_{m=1}^{M_k} B_m(f_j) w_{k,m}^{(\delta)} \label{eq:delta_spline}\\
\Re[\theta_{kl}(f_j)] &= \sum_{m=1}^{M_{kl}} B_m(f_j) w_{kl,m}^{(\Re)} \label{eq:theta_re_spline}\\
\Im[\theta_{kl}(f_j)] &= \sum_{m=1}^{M_{kl}} B_m(f_j) w_{kl,m}^{(\Im)} \label{eq:theta_im_spline}
\end{align}
where $B_m(f_j)$ are B-spline basis functions evaluated at frequency $f_j$, $M_k$ and $M_{kl}$ are the numbers of basis functions for the diagonal and off-diagonal components respectively, and $w_{k,m}^{(\delta)}$, $w_{kl,m}^{(\Re)}$, $w_{kl,m}^{(\Im)}$ are the corresponding spline coefficients.

The B-spline basis functions are constructed using degree-$r$ polynomials with knots placed according to the data characteristics. The flexibility of each component can be controlled by choosing the number of basis functions and the knot placement strategy.

\subsection{Bayesian Inference}
We place hierarchical priors on the spline coefficients to enforce smoothness while allowing for automatic model selection. For each component, we use:
\begin{align}
\bold{w}_j | \phi_j &\sim \mathcal{N}(\bold{0}, (\phi_j \bold{P}_j)^{-1}) \\
\phi_j | \delta_j &\sim \text{Gamma}(\alpha_\phi, \delta_j \beta_\phi) \\
\delta_j &\sim \text{Gamma}(\alpha_\delta, \beta_\delta)
\end{align}
where $\bold{w}_j$ represents the vector of spline coefficients for component $j$, $\bold{P}_j$ is the penalty matrix enforcing smoothness (typically a second or third-order difference matrix), and $\phi_j$, $\delta_j$ are precision parameters that control the amount of smoothing.

This hierarchical structure allows each spectral component to have its own degree of smoothness, automatically adapting to the underlying spectral characteristics. 
The penalty matrices $\bold{P}_j$ are constructed from finite differences of the spline coefficients, penalizing rapid changes and promoting smooth spectral estimates.

The posterior distribution can be sampled using MCMC methods, with the Cholesky parameterization ensuring positive definiteness of the spectral density matrix throughout the sampling process.
In our implementation, we use the No-U-Turn Sampler (NUTS) algorithm, which is well-suited for high-dimensional parameter spaces and efficiently explores the posterior distribution.

To summarise spectral estimation accuracy across frequencies, 
we report the relative integrated absolute error (RIAE)
\begin{equation}

where $\lVert \cdot \rVert_F$ denotes the Frobenius norm. 
The integrals are evaluated numerically using Simpson's rule on the discrete frequency grid 
shared by the theoretical spectrum $S(f)$ and the estimator $\widehat{S}(f)$.

\section{Discussion}

The proposed multivariate P-spline approach provides a flexible and computationally efficient framework for spectral density matrix estimation in multichannel time series, with direct relevance to LISA and other gravitational wave experiments. 
By leveraging the Cholesky parameterization, the method guarantees positive definiteness and enables parallel inference for each channel, significantly reducing computational cost. 
The use of hierarchical shrinkage priors and B-spline bases allows the model to adapt to a wide range of spectral structures, balancing smoothness and fidelity to the data.

Simulation studies confirm that the method achieves accurate recovery of both PSD and cross-spectral components, with uncertainty quantification that reflects the available information. 
The approach is robust to the choice of spline basis size and scales well with data volume, making it suitable for routine application as new data become available.

Future work could extend the model to handle nonstationary or time-varying spectra, incorporate additional physical constraints, or integrate parametric components for known instrumental features. 
Application to real LISA data, including scenarios with significant inter-channel correlations, will further demonstrate the utility of this framework for gravitational wave data analysis.
